# Cross-correlating HI 21-cm and Fermi-LAT gamma-ray maps: a comprehensive guide

The Pinetti, Camera, Fornengo & Regis (2020, arXiv:2001.02649; JCAP 07, 044) framework provides the first theoretical prediction of the angular cross-correlation between HI 21-cm brightness temperature maps and the unresolved gamma-ray background — a powerful multi-messenger probe of dark matter annihilation. With MeerKAT and Fermi-LAT, they forecast a signal-to-noise ratio of **3.7 for astrophysical sources alone**, rising to 5.7 and 8.2 with SKA Phase 1 and Phase 2. The framework rests on a halo-model description of HI calibrated by Padmanabhan, Refregier & Amara (2017), combined with Fermi-LAT gamma-ray window functions for astrophysical emitters and dark matter. This report dissects every layer of the HI modelling and MeerKAT instrumentation underpinning that forecast — the physics, the specific assumptions, how they have held up against five years of subsequent data, and where the dominant uncertainties lie.

---

## Part 1 — Where neutral hydrogen survives after reionization

After reionization completes at z ≈ 6, the intergalactic medium is overwhelmingly ionized (neutral fraction < 10⁻⁴). Surviving neutral hydrogen resides almost exclusively in **self-shielded, overdense gas clouds** — damped Lyman-α systems (DLAs) with column densities N_HI ≥ 2 × 10²⁰ cm⁻² — within the interstellar medium of galaxies hosted by dark matter halos (Wolfe, Gawiser & Prochaska 2005).

**The critical halo mass window.** Pinetti et al. state that halos of mass **5 × 10⁹ to ~10¹² M☉** contain most of the cosmic HI. This range emerges from two physical boundary effects. At the low-mass end, halos below ~10⁸ M☉ have virial velocities v_c ≲ 25–30 km/s. The metagalactic UV photoionization background heats the IGM to T ~ 10⁴ K, raising the Jeans mass and preventing gas from accreting into these shallow potential wells (Rees 1986; Efstathiou 1992; Pontzen et al. 2008). Simulations confirm that halos with v_c < 30 km/s do not efficiently host neutral gas (Bagla, Khandai & Datta 2010). At the high-mass end, halos above ~10¹² M☉ have virial temperatures exceeding ~10⁵–10⁶ K, where infalling gas is shock-heated to the hot X-ray-emitting phase rather than cooling to form a neutral component. AGN feedback in these massive systems further heats and expels gas. The intervening "sweet spot" at 10¹⁰–10¹² M☉ contains halos deep enough to accrete gas despite the UV background, yet cool enough for that gas to settle into a neutral phase. ALFALFA abundance-matching analyses and the conditional HI mass function both confirm this picture (Papastergis et al. 2013; Padmanabhan & Kulkarni 2017).

**The 21-cm hyperfine transition.** The line arises from the spin-flip of the electron in the hydrogen ground state between the triplet (F = 1, parallel spins, statistical weight 3) and singlet (F = 0, anti-parallel, weight 1) hyperfine levels, separated by ΔE = 5.88 × 10⁻⁶ eV. The rest-frame frequency is **ν₂₁ = 1420.405 MHz** (λ = 21.106 cm), and the spontaneous emission coefficient is A₁₀ = 2.85 × 10⁻¹⁵ s⁻¹, corresponding to a natural lifetime of ~10⁷ yr. In the post-reionization regime, the spin temperature T_s greatly exceeds T_CMB via collisional coupling and the Wouthuysen–Field (Lyα pumping) effect, so the 21-cm signal is always seen in emission against the CMB, and the brightness temperature becomes independent of T_s. The observed frequency maps directly to redshift via ν_obs = 1420.405/(1 + z) MHz — the foundation of HI intensity mapping as a three-dimensional probe of large-scale structure (Bharadwaj & Ali 2004; Pinetti et al. Eq. 3.1).

**The HI density parameter Ω_HI(z).** Defined as Ω_HI(z) = ρ_HI(z)/ρ_crit,0, this parameter sets the overall amplitude of the HI signal. In the halo model it is computed as Ω_HI = (1/ρ_crit) ∫ (dn/dM) M_HI(M,z) dM (Pinetti et al. Eq. 3.2). Observational constraints come from multiple probes. Local 21-cm emission surveys such as HIPASS give Ω_HI h₇₀ ≈ 3.8 × 10⁻⁴ at z ~ 0 (Zwaan et al. 2005), while the ALFALFA α.100 catalog gives Ω_HI h₇₀ ≈ (3.9–4.3) × 10⁻⁴ (Jones et al. 2018). DLA absorbers from the SDSS-III sample yield Ω_HI ≈ (0.8–1.0) × 10⁻³ at z ~ 2–3.5 (Noterdaeme et al. 2012), and the GGG survey extends to z ~ 4.9 with Ω_HI = (0.98 ± 0.20) × 10⁻³ (Crighton et al. 2015). Intensity mapping cross-correlations such as GBT × WiggleZ constrain Ω_HI b_HI at z ~ 0.8 (Switzer et al. 2013; Masui et al. 2013).

A commonly used empirical fit is Ω_HI(z) ∝ (1 + z)^0.6, indicating a gradual increase from ~4 × 10⁻⁴ at z = 0 to ~10⁻³ at z ~ 2–5. The cross-correlation signal amplitude C_ℓ^{HI×γ} scales **linearly with Ω_HI** through the mean brightness temperature, so the ~20–30% observational uncertainty in Ω_HI directly propagates into a comparable fractional uncertainty in the predicted signal.

**Mean brightness temperature.** In the post-reionization limit (T_s ≫ T_CMB), the mean 21-cm brightness temperature is (Pinetti et al. Eq. 3.4):

> **T̄_b(z) ≈ 188 h Ω_HI(z) (1 + z)² / E(z) mK**

where E(z) = H(z)/H₀. Typical values are **~55 μK at z = 0**, **~140 μK at z = 1**, and **~240 μK at z = 2**, increasing with redshift because both Ω_HI and (1 + z)²/E(z) grow. These sub-millikelvin amplitudes — four to five orders of magnitude below the astrophysical foregrounds — are the fundamental challenge of intensity mapping.

## Part 2 — The HI–halo mass relation and its parameter space

Pinetti et al. adopt the Padmanabhan, Refregier & Amara (2017) parametric relation (Eq. 3.7):

> **M_HI(M, z) = α f_{H,c} M (M/10¹¹ h⁻¹ M☉)^β exp[−(v_{c,0}/v_c(M, z))³]**

where f_{H,c} = (1 − Y_p) ≈ 0.76 is the cosmic hydrogen mass fraction and v_c(M, z) is the halo circular velocity. Each parameter encodes distinct physics. The **normalization α** controls the overall fraction of baryonic hydrogen in neutral form per halo — it directly scales Ω_HI and hence T̄_b. The **power-law slope β** (negative, with best-fit values around −0.58 to −0.69) encodes the decline of HI efficiency with halo mass: more massive halos convert a smaller fraction of their baryons to neutral gas, reflecting virial shock-heating and AGN feedback. The **minimum circular velocity v_{c,0}** sets the low-mass cutoff: halos with v_c < v_{c,0} have their HI content exponentially suppressed, encoding the photoionization heating that prevents gas accretion in shallow potential wells.

**Calibration.** Padmanabhan et al. (2017) fit these parameters via MCMC (using CosmoHammer) simultaneously to: (i) the ALFALFA HI mass function at z ~ 0 (Martin et al. 2010), (ii) DLA column-density distributions at z ~ 2–5 (Noterdaeme et al. 2012; Crighton et al. 2015), (iii) Ω_HI measurements across z ~ 0–5, (iv) DLA incidence rate dN/dX and DLA bias b_DLA, (v) small-scale clustering from ALFALFA (Martin et al. 2012; Papastergis et al. 2013), and (vi) intensity mapping constraints at z ~ 0.8 (Switzer et al. 2013). The specific values adopted by Pinetti et al. — **α = 0.176, β = −0.69, v_{c,0} = 101.61 km/s** — differ slightly from the Padmanabhan et al. (2017) published best-fits for the altered-NFW profile (α = 0.17, β = −0.55, v_{c,0} = 37.2 km/s) or the exponential profile (α = 0.09, β = −0.58, v_{c,0} = 36.3 km/s), suggesting Pinetti et al. may have adopted a variant parametrization or re-calibrated values. The notably larger v_{c,0} = 101.61 km/s compared to the original ~37 km/s implies a more aggressive low-mass cutoff, placing the HI minimum-mass halo at roughly **10¹⁰ M☉** rather than **~10⁸–10⁹ M☉**. This choice would shift the effective HI bias upward while reducing the total Ω_HI contribution from low-mass halos, and warrants careful verification against the original parameter tables.

**Alternative models.** The principal competitors are cosmological hydrodynamical simulations. **IllustrisTNG** (Villaescusa-Navarro et al. 2018) assigns HI in post-processing using the Rahmati et al. (2013) self-shielding prescription; it agrees well with Ω_HI at z ~ 0 but shows more complex M_HI(M) with significant scatter and environmental dependence (assembly bias) not captured by the parametric model. **SIMBA** (Davé et al. 2019, 2020) uses meshless-finite-mass hydrodynamics with torque-limited BH accretion and bipolar kinetic AGN jets, and tends to produce lower HI masses in intermediate-mass halos than IllustrisTNG due to different AGN feedback prescriptions. **MUFASA** (Davé et al. 2017) is the predecessor of SIMBA with simpler feedback.

All models agree reasonably at z ~ 0 but diverge by factors of **2–5 at z > 1**, particularly in Ω_HI contributions from halos above 10¹² M☉. The CHIME auto-power spectrum interpretation (CHIME Collaboration 2026) found **3.1–4.0σ disagreement** with IllustrisTNG predictions at z ~ 1, likely due to inadequate modelling of nonlinear redshift-space clustering of HI in the simulations. The Padmanabhan parametric model's advantage is its direct calibration to multi-redshift data, making it well-suited for analytic halo-model calculations.

**Sensitivity analysis.** The cross-correlation C_ℓ^{HI×γ} ∝ T̄_b × b_HI × W_γ × P_m on large scales. Since T̄_b ∝ α linearly, **α is the parameter that most directly scales the overall signal amplitude**. The slope β redistributes HI across halo masses, affecting both Ω_HI and the effective b_HI in a correlated but partially cancelling manner. The cutoff v_{c,0} has the most complex effect: increasing it reduces Ω_HI but raises b_HI (by removing low-bias, low-mass halos), so the net impact on the product Ω_HI × b_HI depends on the steepness of the halo mass function. Fisher-forecast analyses (Padmanabhan et al. 2019) show the HI auto-power spectrum is most sensitive to v_{c,0} and β as astrophysical parameters, while the cross-correlation amplitude uncertainty is dominated by the combined product **Ω_HI × b_HI**.

## Part 3 — The HI density profile: a cored distribution far more compact than dark matter

Pinetti et al. model the HI radial density within halos using the altered NFW profile with a thermal core (Eq. 3.9):

> **ρ_HI(r) = ρ₀ r_s³ / [(r + 0.75 r_s)(r + r_s)²]**

This profile was introduced by Maller & Bullock (2004) for multiphase halo gas and subsequently adopted for DLA studies by Barnes & Haehnelt (2010, 2014) and incorporated into the halo model by Padmanabhan & Refregier (2017).

**Why a core, not a cusp.** Unlike collisionless dark matter, which forms the familiar r⁻¹ NFW cusp, neutral hydrogen gas has **thermal pressure support**. The warm neutral medium at T ~ 10⁴ K exerts gas pressure that resists gravitational concentration toward the halo centre, producing a flat core at r ≲ 0.75 r_s. Additionally, gas retains angular momentum during infall, settling into disk-like configurations rather than cuspy spheroids. The profile transitions smoothly to the standard NFW r⁻³ envelope at large radii (r ≫ r_s), preserving consistency with the overall halo structure. The 0.75 r_s core scale arises naturally from thermal equilibrium considerations in multiphase cooling simulations (Cole et al. 2000; Maller & Bullock 2004).

**HI concentration c_HI and why it vastly exceeds the DM value.** Padmanabhan & Refregier (2017) parametrize the HI concentration as c_HI(M, z) = c_{HI,0} (M/10¹¹ M☉)^{−0.109} × 4/(1 + z)^{0.13}, with a best-fit **c_{HI,0} ≈ 139**. This is roughly **10× larger** than typical dark matter concentrations (c_DM ~ 10–20, Macciò et al. 2007). The physical reason is straightforward: baryonic gas can **dissipate energy** through radiative cooling (atomic line emission, Compton cooling), losing kinetic energy and contracting to much smaller radii than collisionless dark matter. For a Milky Way-like galaxy, the HI disk extends to ~15–30 kpc while the virial radius is ~200–350 kpc — a ratio directly mirroring the high c_HI. The weak mass scaling M^{−0.109} reflects a mild trend for lower-mass halos to be relatively more HI-concentrated.

**Normalization** is set by mass conservation: ∫₀^{R_vir} ρ_HI(r) 4πr² dr = M_HI(M, z) (Eq. 3.10), uniquely determining ρ₀ given the profile shape, c_HI, and the HI–halo mass relation.

**Fourier transform and angular scale dependence.** The normalized Fourier transform ũ_HI(k|M, z) = (1/M_HI) ∫ ρ_HI(r) [sin(kr)/(kr)] 4πr² dr determines the 1-halo term contribution to the power spectrum. Because c_HI ≫ c_DM, the HI profile is far more compact and its Fourier transform remains close to unity (ũ ≈ 1) out to much higher k than the DM NFW profile. On **large scales** (low ℓ < ~100), the 2-halo term dominates and both profiles give ũ → 1, so the profile shape is irrelevant. The profile choice becomes significant for the **1-halo term at ℓ ≳ 200–500**, corresponding to k ≳ 0.1–0.3 h Mpc⁻¹ at z ~ 0.3–1. For the Pinetti et al. cross-correlation forecast, which spans ℓ ~ 10–1000, profile details matter primarily at the high-ℓ end.

**Robustness from simulations and observations.** IllustrisTNG shows that mean HI profiles are approximately universal across mass and redshift but exhibit large halo-to-halo scatter (Villaescusa-Navarro et al. 2018). TNG50 produces HI maps that are **less concentrated** than WHISP observations (Gebek et al. 2023), attributed to central HI deficits from the ejective character of SMBH feedback in TNG. Resolved HI observations from the THINGS survey (Walter et al. 2008) and WHISP show that azimuthally averaged profiles flatten or decline near the centre (central depression) and fall exponentially in the outer regions — better described by an **exponential disk** than a modified NFW at low redshift, as acknowledged by Padmanabhan et al. (2017). For the cross-correlation analysis at z ~ 0.1–0.5, the modified NFW remains a reasonable approximation since the profile primarily affects the 1-halo term at high ℓ where instrumental noise is large.

## Part 4 — HI bias: how neutral hydrogen traces large-scale structure

The effective HI bias in the halo model is (Pinetti et al. Eq. 3.6):

> **b_HI(z) = (1/ρ̄_HI) ∫ dM (dn/dM) M_HI(M, z) b(M, z)**

This is a **mass-weighted** average of the halo bias, where the weighting is by HI content rather than number or total mass. Because HI preferentially resides in halos of ~10¹⁰–10¹² M☉, the effective HI bias reflects the clustering properties of these host halos. Very massive halos (>10¹³ M☉), despite being highly biased tracers of structure, contribute little because they contain minimal HI. Very low-mass halos, though numerous, are suppressed by photoionization.

**Predicted and measured values.** From the Padmanabhan model and observations:

| Redshift | b_HI (predicted) | Observational constraint |
|----------|------------------|--------------------------|
| z ~ 0 | 0.65–0.85 | ALFALFA: b_HI ≈ 0.8 (Martin et al. 2012) |
| z ~ 0.4 | 0.8–1.0 | MeerKAT × WiggleZ: Ω_HI b_HI r = (0.86 ± 0.16) × 10⁻³ (Cunnington et al. 2023) |
| z ~ 0.8 | 0.9–1.1 | GBT × WiggleZ: Ω_HI b_HI r = (0.43 ± 0.07) × 10⁻³ (Masui et al. 2013) |
| z ~ 2.3 | 1.5–2.2 | DLA bias: b_DLA = 2.17 ± 0.2 (Font-Ribera et al. 2012) |

The HI bias **increases monotonically with redshift**. At z ~ 0, HI traces relatively underdense regions (blue, late-type galaxies), giving b_HI < 1. By z ~ 2–3, HI-hosting halos become rarer peaks in the density field with higher bias, reaching b_HI ~ 2–3. This evolution is amplified relative to the general galaxy population because the HI-hosting halo mass range shifts upward at higher z.

**Scale dependence.** Linear (scale-independent) bias breaks down at **k ~ 0.3 h Mpc⁻¹** at z ≤ 3 (Villaescusa-Navarro et al. 2018; Castorina & Villaescusa-Navarro 2017). Using the Limber approximation (ℓ ~ k × χ(z)) for z ~ 0.3–1 where χ ~ 1–3 Gpc, this corresponds to ℓ ~ 300–900. For the Pinetti et al. multipole range of ℓ = 10–1000, **ℓ ≲ 100–300 is safely in the linear bias regime**, while **ℓ > 300–500 enters the nonlinear regime** where the 1-halo term and scale-dependent bias become important.

**Impact on the cross-correlation.** On large scales, C_ℓ^{HI×γ} ∝ b_HI linearly, so a 20% uncertainty in b_HI translates directly to a ~20% uncertainty in the astrophysical cross-correlation amplitude. For the dark matter signal, which depends on ρ_DM² rather than b_HI, the uncertainty propagates indirectly through the astrophysical "foreground" that must be subtracted or distinguished. Current constraints on b_HI have 20–50% uncertainties depending on redshift, making it one of the dominant astrophysical systematics alongside M_HI(M, z) and Ω_HI. However, joint measurement of HI auto- and cross-power spectra can break the Ω_HI–b_HI degeneracy (since auto ∝ b_HI² while cross ∝ b_HI).

## Part 5 — Observational validation from ALFALFA through MeerKAT and CHIME

**The ALFALFA survey.** The Arecibo Legacy Fast ALFA survey used the 7-beam ALFA receiver on the 305-m Arecibo telescope to perform a blind extragalactic HI survey over ~7000 deg² at z < 0.06. The final α.100 catalog contains **~31,500 extragalactic HI sources** (Haynes et al. 2018), spanning HI masses from ~10⁶ to ~10¹⁰·⁸ M☉. Martin et al. (2012) and Papastergis et al. (2013) measured the correlation function and power spectrum of HI-selected galaxies, finding that HI sources cluster more weakly than optically selected samples, consistent with b_HI ≈ 0.8 at z ~ 0, and showing no convincing dependence of clustering on HI mass.

**Pinetti et al. Figure 1.** They compare their halo model HI power spectrum prediction to ALFALFA measurements, finding **good agreement on large scales** (k < 1 h Mpc⁻¹) where the 2-halo term dominates, but a systematic **underestimate on small scales** (below ~1 h⁻¹ Mpc). This discrepancy arises because the standard halo model with a single continuous HI profile per halo does not capture satellite galaxy contributions, gas stripping in group environments, or other baryonic physics that redistribute HI on sub-halo scales. Padmanabhan et al. (2023) showed that adding finger-of-God effects causes ≳10% deviations at k ≳ 0.1 h cMpc⁻¹, and that satellite contributions enter primarily at k > 10 h Mpc⁻¹.

**The revolution of 2023–2026.** Since Pinetti et al.'s 2020 paper, the HI intensity mapping field has undergone a transformation from upper limits to high-significance detections:

**MeerKAT** delivered the **7.7σ cross-correlation detection** with WiggleZ galaxies at z ≈ 0.40–0.46 (Cunnington et al. 2023), measuring Ω_HI b_HI r = (0.86 ± 0.16) × 10⁻³. Paul et al. (2023) achieved the first interferometric auto-power spectrum detection at z = 0.32 and 0.44, at 8.0σ and 11.5σ respectively on nonlinear scales (0.3 < k < 8 Mpc⁻¹). Carucci et al. (2025) confirmed the cross-correlation with improved multi-scale PCA cleaning, finding Ω_HI b_HI r = (0.93 ± 0.17) × 10⁻³ at ~6σ. The MeerKLASS deep field (MeerKLASS Collaboration 2025) reached thermal noise of ~1.21 mK — the first time thermal noise is **subdominant to HI fluctuations** at k ≲ 0.15 h Mpc⁻¹.

**CHIME** detected 21-cm emission via stacking on eBOSS catalogs at z = 0.78–1.43 (CHIME Collaboration 2023), achieving 7.1σ (LRG), 5.7σ (ELG), and **11.1σ (QSO)** detections. Cross-correlation with the Lyα forest at z ≈ 2.3 yielded a **9σ detection** — the highest-redshift 21-cm emission measurement to date (CHIME Collaboration 2024). Most remarkably, the **first auto-power spectrum detection at z ~ 1** with **12.5σ significance** was announced in late 2025 (CHIME Collaboration 2025), covering k = 0.4–1.5 h Mpc⁻¹ at z = 1.01–1.34 without reliance on external galaxy surveys.

**FAST** remains in the systematics mitigation phase for cosmological intensity mapping, characterizing 1/f noise (knee frequency ~6 × 10⁻⁴ Hz after SVD subtraction; Hu et al. 2021) and beam patterns, though the FASHI survey is producing local-universe HI galaxy catalogs.

**Does the Padmanabhan model hold up?** The measured values of Ω_HI × b_HI from MeerKAT and CHIME are **broadly consistent** with Padmanabhan et al. (2017) predictions on large scales. Padmanabhan et al. (2023) showed the halo model fits MeerKAT auto-power data at z = 0.32 well, with some evidence for higher-than-expected Ω_HI or b_HI at z = 0.44. However, the CHIME z ~ 1 interpretation paper found **3.1–4.0σ tension** with IllustrisTNG, suggesting that simulations underpredict nonlinear HI clustering in redshift space. The parametric halo model remains a viable framework, but extensions incorporating improved redshift-space distortion modelling and satellite contributions are needed for the precision era.

## Part 6 — MeerKAT as an intensity mapping instrument

**Array configuration.** MeerKAT comprises **64 dishes** of **13.5 m diameter** (offset Gregorian, unblocked aperture) in the Upper Karoo, South Africa. About 70% of dishes lie within a dense 1-km core (Gaussian dispersion ~300 m), with the remainder extending to maximum baselines of ~8 km. Frequency coverage spans the UHF band (580–1015 MHz, HI at z = 0.4–1.45) and L-band (900–1670 MHz, HI at z = 0.0–0.58), each with 4096 frequency channels. In practice, the L-band cosmological window is severely limited by RFI to only **971–1023 MHz** (z ≈ 0.39–0.46), motivating the shift to UHF for MeerKLASS, which provides ~40× larger comoving volume (Cunnington et al. 2025).

**Single-dish versus interferometric mode.** For intensity mapping, these modes access complementary angular scales. In **single-dish (auto-correlation) mode**, each dish operates as a scanning total-power radiometer, measuring large angular scales (ℓ ≲ few hundred) critical for cosmological scales. The scanning strategy has dishes slewing at 5 arcmin/s in azimuth at constant elevation, with noise-diode injection every 20 seconds for relative gain calibration. The combined 64-dish system effectively multiplies integration time by 64. In **interferometric mode**, 2016 baselines from 64 dishes resolve structure on small scales but filter out modes below ℓ_min set by the shortest baseline (~29 m).

**System temperature and noise.** The noise model follows Pinetti et al. Eq. 3.18 for single-dish mode. The system temperature is T_sys = T_inst + T_sky, where **T_inst ~ 18–30 K** (cryogenic receivers; ~18 K at L-band centre per SARAO specifications, ~30 K used in forecasts including spillover) and **T_sky = 60 (300 MHz/ν)^{2.55} K** from Galactic synchrotron emission. At 1 GHz, T_sky ≈ 3.3 K gives T_sys ~ 20–25 K; at 600 MHz, T_sky ≈ 10 K gives T_sys ~ 25–40 K. The thermal noise power spectrum scales as P_N ∝ T_sys² Ω_survey / (N_dish t_obs δν N_pol). Achieved noise levels are 1.2–1.4× the theoretical limit: Wang et al. (2021) reported ~2 mK in the pilot survey, while the MeerKLASS deep field (2025) reached **~1.21 mK**.

**Beam and angular resolution.** The single-dish beam is approximately Gaussian with FWHM ≈ λ/D_dish (Eq. 3.17). At 1 GHz, θ_FWHM ≈ 1.27°; at 700 MHz, θ_FWHM ≈ 1.82°; at 580 MHz, θ_FWHM ≈ 2.19°. The `katbeam` library provides more precise cosine-taper beam models. Frequency-dependent beam structure (chromaticity) couples with foregrounds and can bias signal reconstruction (Spinelli et al. 2021); the `eidos` package provides holographic beam models for more careful treatment.

**Interferometric noise** (Eq. 3.19) depends on the baseline density n(u). At 1 GHz, ℓ_min ≈ 2π × 29 m / 0.3 m ≈ **607**, and ℓ_max ≈ 2π × 8000/0.3 ≈ 167,000. The dense core provides excellent short-baseline sensitivity, while the sparse outer baselines probe very small angular scales. The crossover multipole where interferometric noise beats single-dish noise occurs at **ℓ ~ 200–600**, depending on frequency. For MeerKAT, cosmological scales (k ≲ 0.2 h Mpc⁻¹) are accessible only in single-dish mode; the interferometer covers the nonlinear regime.

**MeerKLASS status update.** The MeerKAT Large Area Synoptic Survey (Santos et al. 2017) originally proposed ~4000 deg² over ~4000 hours. As of early 2026, MeerKLASS has accumulated **>650 hours of UHF-band data** reaching nearly **3000 deg²**, making it the largest spectroscopic survey of large-scale structure in the Southern hemisphere. Under the Extended Large Project (XLP) allocation, the survey targets **10,000 deg² by 2028** with ~2500 total hours. The **MeerKAT+ upgrade** adds 13–16 SKA-design dishes (first antenna handed over February 2024), extending the maximum baseline to ~17 km and increasing raw sensitivity by ~50%. MeerKAT+ will eventually integrate into SKA1-MID (197 dishes total).

## Part 7 — Foregrounds are enormous but spectrally distinguishable

**The scale of the problem.** Astrophysical foregrounds at MeerKAT frequencies are **4–5 orders of magnitude brighter** than the HI cosmological signal. Galactic synchrotron emission dominates, with brightness temperatures of ~1–6 K at high Galactic latitudes at 1 GHz (tens to hundreds of K near the plane), following a spectral index β ≈ −2.5 to −2.9 (Irfan et al. 2022 measured β = −2.55 ± 0.13 at 73 MHz steepening to β = −2.87 ± 0.10 at 1050 MHz from MeerKLASS data). Galactic free-free emission contributes ~1–10 K with a flatter spectral index of β ≈ −2.1. Extragalactic point sources (radio galaxies, AGN) add both clustered and Poisson components. The total foreground brightness is of order Kelvin, versus an HI signal of order **0.1–0.3 mK rms**.

**Why removal is possible.** All dominant foregrounds have **smooth power-law spectra** that vary slowly with frequency, while the cosmological 21-cm signal has rich structure in frequency (because it traces three-dimensional large-scale structure). This spectral smoothness allows the foregrounds to be separated from the signal in frequency space. In interferometric data, the chromatic response of baselines confines foreground power to a **wedge-shaped region** in (k_⊥, k_∥) Fourier space, leaving a "clean window" at high k_∥ for signal extraction. The primary limitations are: (i) **frequency-dependent beam** (chromaticity) that mixes foreground power into signal modes, (ii) **calibration errors** that introduce spectral structure mimicking HI, and (iii) **polarization leakage** combined with Galactic Faraday rotation that creates non-smooth spectral features.

**Removal techniques.** The MeerKAT community employs several approaches. **PCA/SVD (blind)** decomposes the data matrix (frequency × pixel) into eigenmodes, removing the first N_fg = 4–30 modes identified as foregrounds (Cunnington et al. 2023 used N_fg = 30). **Multi-scale PCA** (Carucci et al. 2025) applies PCA with different N_fg at different angular scales, optimizing foreground removal while preserving cosmological signal. **Gaussian Process Regression (GPR)** models data as smooth foreground (long frequency correlation length) plus signal+noise (short correlation length), and is better at recovering radial (k_∥) power spectrum than PCA (Soares et al. 2022). **SDecGMCA** (Gkogkou et al. 2025) jointly performs beam deconvolution and source separation, achieving ≲5% accuracy at ℓ = 10–200 under realistic beam conditions — outperforming standard methods. **Foreground avoidance** is used in interferometric mode (delay spectrum technique; Paul et al. 2023), measuring the power spectrum only in the foreground-free region of Fourier space.

All methods cause some **signal loss**, quantified via the transfer function T(k) = P_recovered(k)/P_injected(k), constructed by injecting mock HI signal into real data, re-running the cleaning pipeline, and measuring the recovery fraction (Switzer et al. 2015; Cunnington et al. 2023). Despite ~50% signal loss on the largest scales, the power spectrum can be reconstructed to **sub-percent accuracy** using this transfer function approach (Cunnington et al. 2023b).

**The cross-correlation advantage for HI × gamma rays.** This is perhaps the most important point for your analysis. Foregrounds in HI maps (Galactic synchrotron, free-free) and foregrounds in Fermi-LAT gamma-ray maps (Galactic diffuse gamma-ray emission from cosmic-ray interactions) have **completely different physical origins** and are therefore largely **uncorrelated**. Foreground residuals in one map do not systematically bias the cross-correlation measurement — they increase variance but not the mean of the cross-power estimate. This has been explicitly demonstrated: Cunnington et al. (2019) showed that foregrounds have little impact on optical spectroscopic cross-correlations with reconstructed cross-power within 8.5% even at scales below beam resolution. Berti et al. (2024) confirmed that foregrounds for the two individual probes are expected to be significantly uncorrelated. The practical success of cross-correlation detections (MeerKAT × WiggleZ at 7.7σ, CHIME × eBOSS at 11.1σ, CHIME × Lyα at 9σ despite foreground residuals 6–10× thermal noise) constitutes strong empirical evidence.

**Spurious cross-correlation risks.** The primary concern is **radio-loud AGN that are also gamma-ray emitters**. The Fermi-LAT 4FGL-DR4 catalog contains 7194 sources, of which >60% are blazars — and essentially all blazars are radio-loud by definition. These objects would create genuine but unwanted correlation unrelated to large-scale structure or dark matter. Mitigation requires: (i) masking all 4FGL source positions and their error ellipses from both maps, (ii) masking known bright radio sources from continuum catalogs (NVSS, SUMSS, VLASS) above a flux threshold, and (iii) statistically estimating the residual contribution from unresolved sources below detection thresholds in both bands using source-count models.

## Part 8 — Turning raw MeerKAT data into science-ready intensity maps

**Calibration for intensity mapping demands extreme precision.** Standard interferometric calibration achieves bandpass stability within ~3% over 3 hours. Intensity mapping requires stability at the **~10⁻⁵ level** to separate the μK HI signal from Kelvin-scale foregrounds. The MeerKLASS single-dish pipeline (Wang et al. 2021) models total-power data as a sum of components: calibrator temperature, diffuse foreground, elevation-dependent terrestrial emission, noise-diode signal, and receiver residual. Calibration uses periodic noise-diode injection (1.8 s every 20 s) for relative gain tracking, with absolute calibration from celestial sources (3C 273, Pictor A) every ~1.5 hours. **1/f noise** — correlated temporal gain fluctuations — is the critical systematic for single-dish mode. Li et al. (2021) measured a knee frequency of ~3 × 10⁻³ Hz after 2-SVD-mode subtraction, implying adequate gain stability over timescales of a few hundred seconds. The 1/f noise is highly correlated in frequency and hence removable via SVD/PCA, but aggressive cleaning risks signal loss. Polarization leakage (Stokes Q, V → I) combined with Faraday rotation introduces non-smooth spectral structure requiring careful correction.

**RFI excision.** Despite the Karoo's radio-quiet zone designation, RFI from telecommunications satellites (especially in L-band ~1150–1300 MHz), distant mobile towers, and aircraft transponders remains significant. The Wang et al. (2021) pipeline uses a 3-round flagging approach: (1) strong RFI via the SEEK package (SumThreshold algorithm), flagging 40–60% across the full L-band but only **5–25% in target cosmological windows**; (2) per-channel outlier removal in time-ordered data; (3) map-based identification of residual weak RFI. The UHF band suffers less severe contamination, with RFI restricted to narrow frequency chunks. Additional tools include Tricolour (GPU-accelerated flagger) and deep learning approaches (R-Net architecture).

**Map-making.** Calibrated time-ordered data (Stokes I in Kelvin) are projected into sky maps using optimal map-making: m̂ = (AᵀN⁻¹A)⁻¹ AᵀN⁻¹d, where A is the pointing matrix and N the noise covariance. The MeerKLASS pilot used 0.3° square pixels in flat-sky approximation; recent work employs Cartesian regridding of spherical maps to avoid wide-sky biases. HEALPix pixelization is used in simulations and some analysis frameworks. Foreground subtraction (PCA/GPR/GMCA) then operates on these frequency-pixel data cubes. The transfer function is computed at this stage via mock signal injection.

**Software ecosystem.** The MeerKAT community has developed a rich set of publicly available tools. **CARACal** (formerly MeerKATHI) is an end-to-end containerized calibration and imaging pipeline for interferometric data, using the Stimela framework (Józsa et al. 2020). **katdal** is a Python data access library for MeerKAT Visibility Format (MVF) files, handling HDF5/RDB chunk stores and conversion to CASA MeasurementSets. **katcali** is the MeerKLASS-specific single-dish calibration pipeline for auto-correlation intensity mapping, handling RFI flagging, noise-diode calibration, absolute calibration, and map-making. **oxkat** provides semi-automated calibration and imaging scripts covering the full workflow from flagging through self-calibration (Heywood 2020). **katbeam** and **eidos** provide primary beam models (simplified cosine-taper and holographic, respectively). **MeerFish** is a Fisher forecast code released alongside Cunnington et al. (2025).

**Public data products.** MeerKLASS has released the **first public HI intensity mapping data cube** from the 2019 pilot survey via meerklass.org/data.html, covering 153° < RA < 172°, −1° < Dec < 8° as calibrated intensity cubes after map-making but before foreground removal. UHF and L-band continuum data releases (Paul et al. 2025; Mangla et al. 2025) provide complementary radio source catalogs. MIGHTEE deep-field data in COSMOS, E-CDFS, ELAIS-S1, and XMM-LSS fields support interferometric intensity mapping analyses.

## Conclusion: a framework that has aged well but needs updating

The Pinetti et al. (2020) framework rests on a solid theoretical foundation that has been broadly validated by the subsequent explosion of observational results. **Three key findings** emerge from this assessment. First, the Padmanabhan et al. (2017) HI halo model remains the most practical analytic framework for cross-correlation predictions, but it requires extensions for the precision era — particularly improved modelling of nonlinear redshift-space distortions, satellite contributions, and the 3.1–4.0σ tension with IllustrisTNG identified by CHIME at z ~ 1. Second, the cross-correlation advantage for foreground mitigation that Pinetti et al. relied upon has been **spectacularly confirmed** by multiple experiments: MeerKAT, CHIME, and GBT cross-correlations all succeed even when auto-correlations remain foreground-dominated. This bodes well for the HI × gamma-ray cross-correlation, where the physical disconnect between radio and gamma-ray foregrounds is even stronger than between radio and optical foregrounds. Third, the dominant uncertainty in the predicted signal amplitude comes from the **product Ω_HI × b_HI**, constrained to ~15–20% at z ~ 0.4 by MeerKAT but still poorly known at z > 1. For your analysis, the specific parameter values adopted by Pinetti et al. (particularly v_{c,0} = 101.61 km/s) should be carefully checked against the original Padmanabhan et al. tables and tested for consistency with the latest MeerKAT and CHIME constraints. The MeerKLASS UHF-band survey, now exceeding 3000 deg², combined with 14 years of Fermi-LAT data (4FGL-DR4), places the first actual measurement of the HI × gamma-ray cross-correlation within realistic reach.
